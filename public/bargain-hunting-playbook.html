Bottom line up front
  1. Bargains come from information gaps. The seller didn't recognize a card (short prints, hidden serial numbers, rookies of breakout players), or the listing is hard to find (misspellings, bad photos, odd ending times). Flippers profit by spotting what others missed.
  2. In bins, the targets are serial-numbered cards, tough parallels, refractors, short-print image variations (confirmed by the code on the card back), cheap autographs, prospects' 1st Bowman cards, and hot rookies dealers haven't repriced.
  3. Online, the targets are flawed listings: misspelled names, auctions ending at 2 a.m., blurry photos, small late-night Whatnot rooms, and cross-platform price gaps (COMC or CollX vs. eBay).
  4. 2026 demand is concentrated. Ohtani leads all athletes in eBay sales. Jordan, Wembanyama and Cooper Flagg lead basketball. Young QBs (Dart, Maye, Daniels) and No. 1 pick Fernando Mendoza drive football. Konnor Griffin, Roman Anthony and Jac Caglianone lead baseball's young names.
  5. The buy decision is arithmetic: real sold comps (including accepted Best Offers), minus fees and shipping, minus the margin you need. Flippers set a maximum price before they look, and they take profits rather than hold.
  6. Yes, they negotiate, at shows and shops alike. Bundle cards, pay cash, show comps, point out flaws politely, shop the last hour, and build a relationship with the shop owner. Don't haggle on a $3 card or open at half the asking price.
Part 01

Digging through value bins

Dealers bring monster boxes priced from a dime to around $20 because bins keep people at the table. Some openly prefer moving quantity over single high-end sales.6 Bins fill up fast: one long-time dime-box buyer says it barely surprises him anymore to find recent products, with parallels and inserts, already in dime bins.7

What diggers pull out

One veteran's rules on The Bench forum make a good template: grab nearly every serial-numbered card and every tough parallel (not the one-per-pack kind) from dime boxes, plus set needs and short prints. Take refractors from quarter boxes. Take autographs from 50-cent and often $1 boxes. Grab $6–10 short prints (Topps Heritage, Turkey Red) whenever they show up in $1 boxes. Buy off-grade vintage on a sliding scale by era.1

Top target

Serial-numbered cards

The numbers are printed on the card, but most people don't take the time to look.2 Some parallels hide their numbering in unexpected places, such as advanced-stat or vintage-stock parallels.12

Top target

Short prints & image variations

Hard to recognize by eye, which is why they end up in bins.2 One blogger's best dime-box find was a 2010 Topps Whitey Ford short-print variation.3 See the code table below.

Top target

Rookies & prospects

Rookies that become stars before a dealer reprices: one digger found a David Ortiz rookie in a dime box.2 At the National, Beckett found rookies, inserts, low-numbered parallels, short prints and variations all in bargain bins.5

Cheap upside

Autographs in $1 bins

Low-demand autos still beat base cards on resale and are a standard 50-cent/$1 box pickup.1

Cheap upside

Stars' base cards

One writer bought Mike Trout and Tom Brady base cards out of a quarter box at a recent show.13 They're cheap but liquid, and good lot filler.

Regional

Local-team players

Show sellers say cards of local-team prospects and popular players attract buyers to a table.56 Diggers in other regions pick them up cheaply.

How to confirm a short print in seconds

Since around 2018, Topps has printed a production code at the bottom of the card back. The last three digits tell base cards from variations, and they change every product and year, so diggers check the code chart for the set they're in.8 Some examples:

Digging technique

  • Sample before you commit. One blogger grabs a couple of random handfuls to see whether a box has anything he needs.3
  • Watch for fake "dollar boxes". Many 50-cent and $1 boxes are really dime-box material at a higher price, like '91 Upper Deck Nolan Ryans for a buck.4
  • Do the first lap fast. The same card can sit at three tables with a 30% price spread, so compare before buying.39
  • Check condition. Pull cards out of toploaders and check all four corners.39
  • Look beyond shows. One guide finds local sources (estate sales, shop bulk bins, community trades) more reliably profitable for beginners than online marketplaces, where sellers have usually checked comps already.53
Part 02

Combing eBay and other platforms

Online, the card is usually identified correctly. The bargain comes from a listing fewer buyers see or trust. One collector's approach is to buy auctions ending at inconvenient times, then relist with better photos and a better ending time.15

Tools that do the scanning

130Point

Free aggregation of eBay sold data, including the price of accepted Best Offers, which eBay hides.24

Card Ladder

Price indexes and trend direction across many auction houses, useful for checking whether you're buying into a dip or a spike.24

Market Movers Deals / Cards Under Comps

Compares live Buy It Now listings to recent average sale prices and surfaces the gaps.26 The catch: it sometimes matches the wrong card, such as a Chrome Black auto compared against a regular Chrome auto, so verify every hit.25

Baseball

Stars & young names

  • Ohtani is No. 1 across all sports. In March 2026 his card market was bigger than Judge, Mantle, Griffey and Skenes combined.28
  • Aaron Judge is the No. 2 baseball seller.28 Paul Skenes, Roman Anthony and Bobby Witt Jr. were trending up.29
  • Konnor Griffin, MLB's top prospect.36 One analyst flags his market as overheated: a PSA 10 card valued around $300–350 a year earlier was at $2,000+.35
  • Jac Caglianone (power sells, per SI) and Samuel Basallo lead the 2026 rookie class alongside Anthony.37
Basketball

Icons & the Flagg class

  • Jordan, LeBron, Kobe remain top sellers; Flagg ranked No. 3 overall in February.30
  • Wembanyama tops cllct's NBA 50. His most-traded graded card, a 2023 Prizm base PSA 10, was running $80–90.38
  • Kon Knueppel sales rose 19% in February.40
  • Flagg's Rookie Debut Patch Auto is the chase in Chrome Updates (see Report 2).
Football

Quarterbacks rule

  • Top-25 eBay sellers included Jaxson Dart, Caleb Williams, Drake Maye and Josh Allen. The most cards graded at PSA in July 2026 were Dart (12,255), Maye (9,805) and Cam Ward (5,519).31
  • Fernando Mendoza, the No. 1 pick and Heisman winner, headlines 2026 rookies.32 CardLines also watches Jeremiyah Love, Carnell Tate, Jordyn Tyson and Jadarian Price.33
  • SI describes the 2026 class as less hyped than recent years and notes receivers sit a tier below QBs in the hobby.34

For bin-diggers this means base and parallel cards of these names are the most liquid cheap pickups. Prospect and rookie names move fastest, but they also swing hardest in both directions.

Part 04

How they decide what to pick up

  1. Know the buy price before you look. One flipper says finding the card is half the battle and knowing the price you must buy at is the other half. Check eBay sold listings first.41
  2. Comp properly. Use 130Point for 30-day sold comps, Card Ladder for trend direction, and the PSA pop report for scarcity. Watch for shill bids and Best Offer sales showing the list price.24
  3. Subtract every cost. eBay takes 13.25% on cards,57 plus shipping (eBay Standard Envelope is $0.69–1.25 for cards under $2058) and supplies. Use the calculator below.
  4. Favor liquidity. Stars and hot rookies sell fastest. Rookie patch autos of even mid-tier players tend to have strong floors.42
  5. Buy at yesterday's prices. Watch trends and buy cards that haven't caught up to a player's news.22 Sell into breakout games and spikes.43
  6. Use the season. Prices are usually highest in-season and lowest in the off-season.44
  7. Don't concentrate. Putting all your flip money into one player is gambling.43
  8. Take profits. Cary Williams of American Arbitrage, who sources low-end cards at shows, shops, flea markets and yard sales, advises taking profits when they're available rather than holding out for more.45
  9. Track everything. Log buy price, sale price, fees and profit for every card. Every buy needs a comp-based reason, not just "I think this guy is good."42

Part 05

Negotiating with card shops and dealers

Haggling at shows is expected rather than rude, and vendors often price high because they know buyers will negotiate.46 Shops work the same way, with an added relationship angle.

Tactics that work

Bundle

The more cards you buy from one dealer, the better the chance of a percentage off everything.47 Combine a showcase card with some bin cards into one offer.48 A sample line: "These three come to $105, would you take $90 cash?"49

Pay cash

Many dealers knock 5–10% off for cash automatically.39 Card readers cost vendors 2.5–3.5%, so a number agreed in cash may go back up if you pull out a card.50

Show comps

If a dealer asks $150 for a card that sells for $90 on eBay, show the comp and negotiate down.51

Point out flaws, politely

Something like "this corner is a little soft, could you do [offer]?" is a standard move.52 A "cash plus card" offer can bridge a gap.48

Shop the last hour

The end of a show is when dealers want to avoid packing inventory back up, and when other dealers shop.3955

Ask for their best

"What's your best?" gets the dealer's bottom line without you naming a number first.50

How far below the ask?

Guides disagree. One recommends opening 10–25% under the asking price, not 50%.50 Another suggests 30–40% below on cards you return to later in the show.51 Show etiquette guides agree on the limits: offering 50% of the sticker is insulting, negotiating over a $3 card isn't worth it because the margin isn't there, and you shouldn't hold a card for 20 minutes while you think.54 One guide sets the threshold at negotiating anything over $20 and bundling cheaper cards.39

Working your LCS

Become a regular

Shop owners give regulars advance notice on hot products and deals,59 and relationships lead to preferred pricing.60 From the first debrief: tell the shop who you collect so they set cards aside.

Know their buy side

Shops pay roughly 50–60% of market value in cash, maybe 70% in store credit. eBay nets about 80–87% after fees and shipping.61 That spread is the shop's margin and the room you have when buying from them.

Use store credit as leverage

When you sell to a shop, credit offers typically run 25–50% higher than cash,62 so trading in duplicates for credit stretches your buying power at that shop.

Why dealers bend. A dealer who pays too close to resale can't stay open,63 and unsold inventory ties up cash that could cover payroll or restocking.64 That's why the quickest sales are worth a discount to them, and why bundled cash offers work.
